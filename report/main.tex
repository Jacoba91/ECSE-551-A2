\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Image classification is a fundamental problem in computer vision, 
with the objective of assigning semantic categories to images 
based on their visual content. This work investigates the 
performance of different deep learning architectures on the 
RIVAL10 dataset, consisting of 21,098 labeled images and 5,286 
unlabeled test images across ten approximately balanced object 
categories. The dataset also provides object segmentation masks, 
enabling an analysis of the extent to which classifiers rely on 
object features rather than contextual background information.

The study is divided into two tasks. In Task 1, a Convolutional 
Neural Network (CNN) and a Vision Transformer (ViT) are designed 
and trained from scratch. Architectural choices and training 
hyperparameters are explored experimentally, and the resulting 
models are compared in terms of classification performance, 
generalization, class-specific errors, and reliance on object 
and background features. In Task 2, the objective is to maximize 
classification performance by leveraging transfer learning. 
A frozen, pre-trained DINOv3 Vision Transformer backbone is 
employed as a feature extractor alongside a trainable linear 
classification head.

The experimental results demonstrate that the CNN substantially 
outperforms the ViT when trained from scratch, achieving 
accuracies of [XX]\% and [XX]\%, respectively. This observation 
is consistent with the stronger spatial inductive biases of 
CNNs, which are particularly advantageous when training data 
is limited. The DINOv3-based model considerably outperforms 
both architectures, achieving an accuracy of [XX]\%, 
highlighting the effectiveness of visual representations learned 
through large-scale self-supervised pre-training.

\section{Datasets}
\label{sec:datasets}

\subsection{Dataset Overview}

The RIVAL10 dataset used in this study consists of 21,098
labeled images and 5,286 unlabeled test images, distributed
across ten object categories: bird, car, cat, deer, dog,
equine, frog, plane, ship, and truck. Each image is
accompanied by a segmentation mask distinguishing the
foreground object from the background. These masks enable
controlled perturbation experiments to investigate the
extent to which the classifiers rely on object-specific
features rather than contextual information.

\subsection{Statistical Analysis}

The dataset is approximately class-balanced, with the
number of labeled images per class ranging from 2,011
(truck) to 2,127 (frog). This relatively small variation
suggests that class imbalance is unlikely to substantially
bias the classifiers toward particular categories.
Consequently, no class weighting or oversampling was applied.

The RGB channel statistics were computed exclusively from
the training partition and are presented in
Table~\ref{tab:channel_statistics}. The blue channel exhibits
a lower mean intensity and slightly greater variability
than the red and green channels. Channel-wise normalization
accounts for these differences, ensuring that the input
channels operate on comparable numerical scales during
optimization.

\subsection{Training, Validation, and Test Splits}

The labeled dataset was partitioned into training and
validation sets using a stratified 90/10 split with a
fixed random seed of 42. The resulting partitions contain
18,987 training images and 2,111 validation images.
Stratification preserves the original class proportions,
ensuring that all categories are adequately represented
during both training and validation.

The 5,286 unlabeled competition images constitute the
external test set. Identical dataset partitions were used
across all three architectures to ensure consistent
evaluation and meaningful performance comparisons.

\subsection{Data Preprocessing and Augmentation}

All images were converted to RGB and standardized to an
input dimension of $224 \times 224 \times 3$. Pixel
intensities were converted to floating-point values in
$[0,1]$ and subsequently normalized independently for each
channel according to

\begin{equation}
    \hat{x}_c = \frac{x_c - \mu_c}{\sigma_c},
\end{equation}

where $\mu_c$ and $\sigma_c$ denote the mean and standard
deviation of channel $c$, respectively. For Task 1, these
statistics were computed solely from the training images
to prevent information leakage. For Task 2, the
normalization statistics associated with the pre-trained
DINOv3 checkpoint were used to maintain consistency with
its expected input distribution.

To improve generalization and mitigate overfitting, the
following stochastic transformations were applied during
training:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Random resized cropping:} Crops covering
    80--100\% of the original image area, with aspect
    ratios between 0.9 and 1.1, were resized to
    $224 \times 224$ pixels to introduce variation in
    object scale and spatial composition.

    \item \textbf{Random horizontal flipping:} Images
    were flipped with a probability of 0.5 to reduce
    sensitivity to horizontal orientation.

    \item \textbf{Color jitter:} Brightness, contrast,
    and saturation were varied using a magnitude of 0.2,
    with hue varied using a magnitude of 0.05, to improve
    robustness to changes in illumination and color.
\end{itemize}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]
        {augmentation_comparison.png}
    \caption{Comparison of unaugmented and augmented
    training images across three representative classes.}
    \label{fig:augmentation}
\end{figure}

As illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:augmentation}, these
transformations introduce meaningful geometric and
photometric variation while preserving recognizable
class-specific features. This effectively increases the
diversity of training observations without requiring
additional labeled data.

Validation and test images underwent only deterministic
bilinear resizing, pixel scaling, and channel-wise
normalization. Excluding stochastic transformations
during evaluation ensures that measured performance
differences are attributable to the models rather than
random variations in their inputs.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}

\subsection{Task 1: Convolutional Neural Network}
\label{sec:cnn}

\subsubsection{Model Architecture}

Two CNN architectures, denoted CNN1 and CNN2, were
implemented from scratch using PyTorch. Both architectures
consist of three convolutional blocks with progressively
increasing channel dimensions of 32, 64, and 128.
CNN1 follows a conventional three-layer design, with one
convolutional layer per block, while CNN2 extends this
design to two convolutional layers per block.

Each convolution employs a $3 \times 3$ kernel with
unit stride and padding, preserving spatial dimensions
within each block. Convolutions are followed by batch
normalization and ReLU activation. Each block concludes
with a $2 \times 2$ max pooling operation, reducing the
spatial dimensions by a factor of two.

Following feature extraction, global average pooling
reduces each of the 128 feature maps to a single scalar.
The resulting feature vector passes through a dropout
layer and a fully connected classification layer,
producing ten class logits.

Table~\ref{tab:cnn_architecture} summarizes the structural
differences between the two architectures.

\begin{table}[htbp]
    \centering
    \caption{Comparison of CNN Architectures}
    \label{tab:cnn_architecture}
    \begin{tabular}{lccc}
        \toprule
        Stage & CNN1 & CNN2 & Output Dimensions \\
        \midrule
        Input & -- & -- & $3 \times 224 \times 224$ \\
        Block 1 & 1 Conv & 2 Conv & $32 \times 112 \times 112$ \\
        Block 2 & 1 Conv & 2 Conv & $64 \times 56 \times 56$ \\
        Block 3 & 1 Conv & 2 Conv & $128 \times 28 \times 28$ \\

\subsubsection{Architectural Design Decisions}

The convolutional architecture exploits locality and
weight sharing, providing spatial inductive biases that
are particularly advantageous when training on relatively
limited datasets. Small $3 \times 3$ kernels allow the
network to learn local visual patterns, while successive
convolutional blocks combine these patterns into
increasingly abstract feature representations.

The number of feature channels doubles after each
downsampling stage to increase representational capacity
as spatial resolution decreases. Max pooling reduces
computational cost and provides robustness to small
spatial variations. Batch normalization promotes stable
optimization, while ReLU introduces the nonlinearity
necessary to learn complex feature representations.

Global average pooling was selected instead of directly
flattening the final feature maps to substantially reduce
the number of classifier parameters and mitigate
overfitting. This encourages classification based on
the presence of learned features rather than their
precise spatial locations.

CNN1 served as the initial architectural prototype, while
CNN2 was developed to increase feature extraction capacity
before spatial information is discarded through pooling.
The additional convolutional layers increase the effective
receptive field and introduce further nonlinear
transformations without requiring additional downsampling.
Despite approximately tripling the parameter count,
CNN2 remains relatively lightweight at 289,194 trainable
parameters.

Both architectures were incorporated into preliminary
small-scale training protocols for development and
pipeline verification. CNN2 was subsequently selected
for full-scale training and hyperparameter exploration
based on its greater representational capacity.


\subsection{Task 1: Vision Transformer}
\label{sec:vit}

\subsubsection{Model Architecture}

A Vision Transformer (ViT) was designed and trained
from scratch using PyTorch. The architecture consists
of a learnable patch embedding layer, learnable
positional embeddings, a classification token, four
transformer encoder blocks, and a linear classification
head.

Unlike the CNN, which processes images through successive
spatial convolutions, the ViT represents each image as
a sequence of embedded patches. Given an input image
of dimensions $H \times W \times 3$, a patch size $P$,
and a stride $S$, the number of extracted patches is

\begin{equation}
    N =
    \left(\left\lfloor\frac{H-P}{S}\right\rfloor+1\right)
    \left(\left\lfloor\frac{W-P}{S}\right\rfloor+1\right).
\end{equation}

Patch extraction and linear projection are implemented
jointly using a two-dimensional convolution with kernel
size $P$, stride $S$, and $D$ output channels, where
$D$ denotes the embedding dimension. This operation is
equivalent to flattening each patch and applying a shared
learnable linear projection from $3P^2$ to $D$ dimensions,
while providing a computationally efficient implementation.

The resulting spatial feature maps are flattened into
a sequence of $N$ patch embeddings. A learnable
classification token is prepended to this sequence,
and learnable positional embeddings are added
element-wise, yielding

\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{Z}_0 =
    [\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{cls}};
    \mathbf{x}_1;\ldots;\mathbf{x}_N]
    + \mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{pos}},
\end{equation}

where $\mathbf{x}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{D}$ represents
the embedding of patch $i$ and
$\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{pos}} \in
\mathbb{R}^{(N+1)\times D}$ represents the positional
embedding matrix. Both the classification token and
positional embeddings are initialized to zero and
optimized jointly with the remaining model parameters.
Dropout is applied to the resulting token sequence
before it enters the transformer encoder.

Each encoder block consists of multi-head self-attention
followed by a feedforward network with GELU activation.
Pre-layer normalization and residual connections are
employed around both components to promote stable
gradient propagation. Self-attention allows each token
to incorporate information from all other tokens,
capturing relationships between spatially distant
regions of the image.

Following the final encoder block, the classification
token is extracted, normalized, and passed through
a linear classification head to produce ten class logits.

Table~\ref{tab:vit_architecture} summarizes the baseline
architectural configuration.

    \label{tab:vit_architecture}
    \begin{tabular}{lc}
        \toprule
        Architectural Parameter & Value \\
        \midrule
        Input resolution & $224 \times 224 \times 3$ \\
        Patch size & $16 \times 16$ \\
        Patch stride & 16 \\
        Number of patches & 196 \\

        \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
\end{table}

\subsubsection{Architectural Design Decisions}

The patch-based representation was selected to make
self-attention computationally tractable for image
classification. Applying self-attention directly to
individual pixels would produce an excessively long
token sequence, whereas patch embeddings provide a
more compact representation of local image regions.

The initial implementation exposed patch size and
stride as independent architectural parameters to
support experimentation with overlapping patches.
Overlapping patches were considered as a means of
preserving spatial continuity and reducing information
loss at patch boundaries. However, decreasing the
stride increases the number of tokens, and the
computational and memory costs associated with standard
self-attention grow quadratically with sequence length.
Given the available computational resources, overlapping
patch configurations were not explored. Instead, the
stride was set equal to the patch size, following the
conventional non-overlapping patch formulation.

A baseline patch size of $16 \times 16$ was selected
to retain sufficient spatial detail while maintaining
a manageable sequence length. A coarser patch size
of $32 \times 32$ was also included in the experimental
design to investigate the trade-off between spatial
granularity and computational efficiency.

Positional embeddings were incorporated because
self-attention does not inherently encode the spatial
ordering of input tokens. Learnable positional
embeddings allow the model to distinguish patches
according to their original image locations and
learn task-specific spatial representations.

The classification token provides a dedicated mechanism
for aggregating information across the patch sequence.
Through successive self-attention operations, this
token learns a global image representation suitable
for classification without requiring an explicit
pooling operation over the patch embeddings.

The encoder depth, embedding dimension, and
feedforward dimension were selected to balance
representational capacity against computational cost
and the risk of overfitting. Four attention heads
allow the model to attend to different feature
relationships in parallel, while dropout provides
regularization throughout the architecture.
